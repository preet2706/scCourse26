# Perturb-CITE-seq Melanoma Screen: Discussion of the Three Analysis Tasks

The three analyses discussed below were carried out on a single pooled CRISPR-knockout Perturb-seq screen in human melanoma cells (Frangieh et al., 2021). The data comprise roughly 218,000 individual cells, each profiled simultaneously for its whole transcriptome and for a 24-marker panel of surface proteins, and each belonging to one of three experimental arms: an unstimulated control, a population exposed to interferon-γ, and a tumour–immune co-culture. Across the screen 248 genes were individually knocked out, with about three guide RNAs per gene, alongside non-targeting controls. A preceding quality-control step confirmed that the distributed data were already lightly filtered, left essentially all cells intact, and produced the normalised, dimensionality-reduced expression matrix on which everything that follows is based.

---

The first task centers around the question, whether, given nothing but the expression profile of a single cell, restricted to the 2,000 most variable genes, a classifier can recover which of the three experimental arms that cell came from. Four models of increasing flexibility, logistic regression, a random forest, gradient-boosted trees, and a small neural network, were trained on an identical 80/20 split so that their performance could be compared directly, with cross-validation on the training portion as an additional check.

All four models yielded almost the same result, classifying held-out cells with between 98.2 and 98.9 percent accuracy and a macro-averaged F1 score around 0.98 to 0.99, against a majority-class baseline of roughly 40 percent. Gradient-boosted trees were marginally ahead and the neural network marginally behind, but the differences are negligible, and cross-validation scores tracked the test scores to within a fraction of a percent, which rules out overfitting. Because such uniformly high accuracy can be a symptom of data leakage or a technical confounder rather than of biology, several controls were run. Retraining on deliberately shuffled labels drove accuracy down to chance, confirming that the pipeline itself was sound. Fifty genes drawn at random classified cells at 59 to 75 percent, whereas the fifty most informative genes alone reached 98.4 percent, which shows that the signal is concentrated in a compact and coherent set rather than diffused evenly across the genome, and diffuse spreading is exactly the pattern a batch effect would produce. Those most informative genes, among them GBP1, GBP2 and GBP4, IDO1, IRF1, several HLA genes, CD74, and the chemokines CXCL9, CXCL10 and CXCL11, together form a recognisable interferon and antigen-presentation programme, and an independent differential-expression test returned essentially the same list.

Biologically the interpretation is straightforward. Exposure to interferon-γ, and the more complex signalling milieu of tumour–immune co-culture, leave a deep and highly reproducible imprint on the melanoma transcriptome. Interferon signalling upregulates antigen processing and presentation through MHC class I and II and the invariant chain CD74, induces the anti-proliferative and tryptophan-catabolising enzyme IDO1 along with the guanylate-binding protein GTPases, and drives secretion of the chemokines that recruit T cells. This is precisely the regulatory axis that determines whether a tumour is immunologically "hot" or "cold" and how it is likely to respond to checkpoint blockade.

---

The second task set experimental condition aside and looked within the control arm only, asking which of the knocked-out genes produce similar transcriptional consequences and whether unsupervised clustering can recover biologically meaningful groups among them. Each perturbation was reduced to a single signature, defined as its mean expression profile across the variable genes minus the mean profile of the non-targeting-control cells. Perturbations represented by fewer than fifteen cells in the control arm were dropped, which removed nine genes and left 239 signatures to cluster. Three approaches were compared: Ward hierarchical clustering, k-means, and graph-based Leiden community detection, each with its number of clusters or its resolution fixed by a silhouette criterion rather than chosen by hand. An explicit methodological decision underlies the whole analysis, namely that clustering on raw signature magnitude is dominated by a handful of very strong knockouts, so each signature was first standardised individually. This makes the procedure sensitive to the shape of a transcriptional response rather than its amplitude.

Hierarchical clustering and k-means both settled cleanly on four clusters and agreed closely on their membership, while Leiden found no interior silhouette optimum and collapsed to the coarsest split it was offered, which reflects a known bias of the silhouette measure toward few well-separated groups rather than evidence that the data contain only two communities. The one finding that survived every method is a tight group of roughly 17-22 core-essential genes, comprising translation factors such as EEF1G and EIF4A1, spliceosome and proteasome subunits, an oxidative-phosphorylation component, and cell-cycle drivers including MYC, CCND1 and CDK6. This group was recovered almost by all three algorithms, formed a single strongly anti-correlated block in the raw correlation map, and dominated magnitude-based clustering. Beyond it, the further subdivision of the remaining genes into groups of roughly one hundred, seventy and fifty was only partly coherent: some genuine pathway logic emerged, such as MHC class I genes grouping with the JAK–STAT signalling genes that induce them, but so did clear inconsistencies, such as the HLA family itself being split across two clusters.

The essential-gene cluster is best understood not as a pathway but as a generic stress and growth-arrest response. Removing any component of the machinery a cell needs in order to translate protein or to divide produces a large but non-specific transcriptional collapse that looks much the same whichever essential gene was targeted. This is why the cluster is at once the most robust and the least informative feature of the data, and why it has to be isolated before magnitude-blind clustering can resolve anything more subtle. The natural improvement would be more cells per perturbation and, above all, extension of the analysis into the interferon-γ and co-culture arms, where many of these genes act specifically as regulators of the immune response and would be expected to separate far more cleanly than they do in resting cells. Testing the clusters formally against curated pathway annotations, rather than reasoning from gene identity alone, would convert the present qualitative reading into a quantitative enrichment result.

---

The third task deals with the prediction of a vector of log2 fold-changes across the 2,000 variable genes for a perturbation not present in the trainig data. Working again only in the control arm, fifty perturbations were used for model fitting and a disjoint set of twenty were held out, both drawn so as to span the clusters identified in the previous task. Each target gene was described purely by features computable from unperturbed cells, specifically its co-expression relationship with every other gene together with a small number of summary statistics such as baseline expression, detection rate and variability. Every model was benchmarked against a deliberately uninformative baseline that ignores gene identity entirely and predicts the average fold-change profile observed across the training perturbations. Predictions were scored for each held-out gene by the Pearson correlation with the observed profile, which captures whether the pattern of the response is right, and by the root-mean-square error, which captures whether its magnitude is right, with confidence intervals obtained by resampling the cells of each perturbation.

The naive baseline achieved a mean correlation of about 0.78 and a mean error of about 0.072 log2 units, and no model improved on it: the k-nearest-neighbour and neural-network predictors, with or without the summary statistics, all fell between 0.77 and 0.78 in mean correlation and between 0.071 and 0.074 in error. The median correlation was closer to 0.85, the mean being pulled down by a few barely expressed non-coding targets that were predicted at around 0.4. Most tellingly, the confidence intervals of every model overlapped the baseline's not only on average but for every individual held-out perturbation.

The biology behind this is that most of what makes a knockout's transcriptome predictable is a response that knockouts hold in common, consisting of the loss of the targeted transcript itself, compensating shifts in co-regulated genes, and a mild shared stress response, and in resting melanoma cells that common component dominates. The gene-specific effect, the part that would actually distinguish one knockout from another, is real but small, because in the unstimulated state most regulatory genes are not sufficiently active for their loss to register in the transcriptome.
